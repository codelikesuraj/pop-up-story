Blue Across the Water

An indigo dyer and a kite-maker who lived on opposite shores of a lagoon, and the conversation they held for a whole season without ever saying a word.

The lagoon at the end of the dry season
Chapter One · The near shore

Ireti's Blue Hands

On the near shore of the lagoon lived Ireti, a dyer. All day she worked over clay pots of indigo, folding and tying cloth, stitching it with raffia and painting it with cassava paste so the dye would leave patterns where it could not reach.

Her hands were blue to the wrist, and no amount of scrubbing would change it. Each morning she climbed to her flat roof and hung the new cloth to dry, rows of blue facing the water. Nobody on her side of the lagoon ever looked at them for long.

Chapter Two · The far shore

Folarin's Workshop

Across the water, where the palms leaned over the mud, lived Folarin, who made kites. He split bamboo thinner than a fingernail, glued paper over the frames and tied tails of rag that snapped in the wind.

Most mornings he tested a kite above the lagoon. And most mornings, while it climbed, he found himself looking past it at a rooftop on the near shore, where rows of blue cloth came out with the sun. He did not know whose they were. He only knew he waited for them.

Chapter Three · An answer in the sky

The First Kite

One evening Folarin painted a kite in indigo, with ringed circles copied as well as he could from a cloth he had stared at all week. The next morning he flew it high over the lagoon, straight toward her roof.

Ireti looked up from her pots and saw her own pattern hanging in the sky. She laughed out loud, alone on the roof, the first time anyone had answered her work. She did not know his name. But she knew someone over there had been looking.

iii
Her own pattern, flying back to her
Chapter Four · The dry season

A Conversation in Blue

So it began. Ireti dyed a cloth of zigzags like waves, and the next day a zigzag kite rose from the far shore. She answered with dots like rain. He sent up a kite with a tail of rain. When a storm tore one of his kites, she hung out a cloth stitched with mending lines.

For a whole season they spoke this way, a cloth at dawn and a kite by noon. The fishermen in between began to read it too. Neither lover crossed the water. It felt too fine a thing to risk spoiling by speaking.

Waves answered with waves, rain with rain
Chapter Five · The dust wind

The Harmattan

Then the harmattan came down from the north. Fine dust filled the air until the far shore vanished and the sun hung pale as a coin. Ireti hung her best cloth every morning and could not tell if anyone saw it. Folarin flew kite after kite into a sky that swallowed them.

Day after day there was no answer. Each began to fear the other had grown tired of the game. Ireti stopped climbing to the roof. On the far shore, Folarin sat among his unflown kites and wondered if a season of patterns had meant anything at all.

The far shore disappears
Chapter Six · Too big to miss

The Last Kite

At last Folarin built the biggest kite of his life, taller than himself. He tied every scrap of string he owned into one line, waited for a strong wind, and let it run out over the water until the line ended and the kite flew free.

It came down in the dust on Ireti's roof. On it he had painted no pattern this time, only a picture: a small canoe in the middle of the lagoon, with two people in it. Ireti held it in her blue hands for a long time. Then she went down to the water.

Chapter Seven · Two empty houses

Passing in the Haze

Ireti pushed off in her neighbour's canoe and rowed toward the far shore. She could see nothing but dust. She did not know that Folarin, unable to bear waiting for an answer, had already launched his own canoe and was rowing toward her.

In the thick of the haze they passed each other, a stone's throw apart, each hearing only the splash of a stranger's paddle. Ireti reached an empty workshop full of kites. Folarin reached an empty roof full of blue cloth. And each sat down in the other's doorway to wait.

vii
Each heard only a stranger's paddle
Chapter Eight · Meeting in the middle

Halfway

By evening the neighbours on both shores were laughing. "Your dyer is sitting at the kite-maker's door," the fishermen shouted across. "And your kite-maker is sitting at hers!"

So, at the same moment, both of them pushed off again. This time neither aimed for the other's house. They rowed for the middle of the lagoon, where the painted kite had put them. As the wind turned and the dust began to clear, they saw each other at last.

Afterword · The festival of blue kites

What the Story Keeps

They married that same dry season, with a cloth of ringed circles for her wrapper and a kite of ringed circles flying over the feast. Ever since, when the harmattan clears, the lagoon town flies blue kites, and the dyers hang out their best cloth to answer them.

  • ◇Love is noticing. It began with one person looking closely at another's work.
  • ◇Love is a conversation. Each pattern was an answer to the last one.
  • ◇Silence is not an answer. In the haze, both assumed the worst and were both wrong.
  • ◇Someone has to cross the water, and it works best when both set out to meet in the middle.
