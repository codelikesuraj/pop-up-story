The Keeper's Light

A lighthouse keeper, a lens-maker, and the night the sea came for their town, the same night their daughter was born.

Chapter One · The Point

The Keeper and the Lens-Maker

At the end of the harbour town, on a finger of black rock called the Point, stood a striped lighthouse. Its keeper was Kayode, who could row faster than any fisherman and climb all hundred and twelve steps without stopping for breath.

His wife, Amara, made the lens. She ground glass by hand into rings that caught the smallest flame and threw it miles out to sea. Every night the fishing fleet sailed out by that light, and every morning it brought them home.

Chapter Two · The storm

The Night She Was Born

Their daughter came early, on the wildest night anyone could remember. Rain hammered the shutters of the keeper's cottage and the wind made the tower hum like a struck bell. Amara named her Ifeoluwa, and they called her Ife.

But Kayode could not stop looking out to sea. The whole fleet was still out there, forty boats with half the town aboard, and somewhere beyond the reef they were steering by his light.

Chapter Three · Midnight

The Light Goes Out

Just after midnight a wave taller than the tower struck the Point. It burst the lantern windows, drowned the flame, and smashed Amara's lens into a thousand pieces across the stones.

Kayode climbed through the spray to the top. Only the heart of the lens had survived, a disc of glass no bigger than a hand. Without the light, the fleet would run onto the reef in the dark, and the surge behind them would reach the town before morning.

The lantern shatters
Chapter Four · Before dawn

The Choice

Beyond the reef stood the Outer Rock, where the first keepers had burned a fire long before the tower was built. A beacon lit there would show the fleet the one safe channel. But whoever lit it would be standing in the path of the surge when it came.

Amara would not let him go alone. The heart of the lens needed a steady hand while the fire was fed. So they wrapped Ife in her mother's red shawl and carried her through the rain to Mama Yemi, the old harbourmaster.

Kayode hung his brass keeper's key around the baby's neck. "Keep it until you need it," he said. Then they walked down to the boat together and did not look back, because they knew that if they did, they could not go.

Chapter Five · The beacon

The Outer Rock

They rowed through waves that stood up like walls, and somehow they reached the Outer Rock. In a hollow at its top lay the old beacon bowl. Kayode broke up the boat's own seats for fuel and soaked them in lamp oil, and Amara raised the heart of the lens behind the flame.

Far out in the dark, forty boats were running straight for the reef.

Chapter Six · Before dawn

The Last Song

All night they fed the fire, and boat after boat slipped through the channel into the harbour. Towards dawn the oil ran out, and Kayode broke up the last of their own boat to keep the flame alive. There was no way back now, and they both knew it.

So Amara held the lens, and Kayode held Amara, and they sang the old keeper's song across the water toward the harbour, where one small window still glowed in Mama Yemi's house. They were still singing when Bayo's boat, the last of the forty, crossed the harbour mouth.

Then the surge came over the Outer Rock, and the light went out.

Chapter Seven · The quay

The Morning After

When the sea fell calm, the quay was full. Forty crews climbed out of forty boats into the arms of their families. Mothers held their sons, children rode on their fathers' shoulders, and someone rang the harbour bell for joy.

Mama Yemi stood at the far edge of it all with the baby in her arms, watching the empty water past the reef. Old Bayo, soaked to the bone, came and stood beside her. He did not say anything. He took off his cap, held it to his chest, and stayed.

Chapter Eight · Growing up

The Story Told Wrong

No one in the fleet had seen who lit the fire on the Outer Rock. They called it a miracle, the ghost-fire of the old keepers come back to save them. But people remembered Kayode and Amara rowing away from the dark tower that night, and since their boat was never found, they said the keepers had fled the storm and drowned running.

Mama Yemi told the truth to anyone who would listen. But she was only an old woman with a baby, and a miracle makes a better story than two neighbours.

Ife grew up hearing it. Some evenings she climbed the dark tower with the brass key around her neck and asked the Outer Rock the question she could never ask anyone else: why did you leave me?

The dark tower, sixteen years on
Chapter Nine · The box

What Mama Yemi Kept

When Ife was sixteen she finally asked her question out loud. Mama Yemi was quiet for a long time. Then she took a box from under her bed. Inside, wrapped in a faded red shawl, lay the heart of a lens. "Bayo found it at the foot of the Outer Rock at low tide," she said. "It was still warm."

She told Ife everything: the shattered lantern, the boat broken up for firewood, the song carried across the water. "They did not leave you, child. They went so that every other child in this town would have someone come home. And they left you the key, so that one day you could climb the stairs after them."

Ife wrapped her mother's shawl around her shoulders, held the warm glass to her chest, and cried for the first time for her parents, instead of at them.

Chapter Ten · The second storm

The Light Comes On

That same night a storm came down on the town like the old one, with the fleet still at sea. Ife ran to the Point with the shawl around her shoulders and her father's key in her hand, climbed all hundred and twelve steps without stopping, and set the heart of the lens behind a new flame.

For the first time in sixteen years, the light came on. It swept over the reef, and over the boats as they turned for home, and over the Outer Rock, where for one heartbeat Ife was sure she saw two figures standing in the spray, side by side. Then the beam moved on, and it was only rock.

Afterword · The night of lanterns

The Night of Lanterns

When the fleet came in, old Bayo climbed onto a crate on the quay and told the whole town what he had seen and found that night, and what Mama Yemi had been saying for sixteen years. Nobody whispered after that. Ife became the keeper of the Point.

Now, every year on the night of the great storm, the town sets paper lanterns on the water, one for every boat that came home, and two more that drift out toward the Outer Rock. Ife lights those two herself.
