Icarus

The boy who flew too close to the sun, and the father whose genius gave him wings.

Drawn from Ovid's Metamorphoses, Book VIII
The Icarian Sea
Chapter One · Athens

A Jealous Genius

Daedalus was the greatest inventor in Athens. His statues seemed to breathe and his tools made work easy. Then his young nephew and apprentice, Perdix, began to outshine him. The boy studied a fish's spine and made the first saw, then joined two iron arms and made the first compass.

Envy ate at Daedalus. On the heights of the Acropolis he pushed the boy over the edge. Athena caught Perdix as he fell and turned him into a partridge, a bird that nests low and never flies high, as if it remembers falling. Daedalus was condemned and fled the city.

Chapter Two · Crete

The Monster and the Maze

Daedalus found shelter with King Minos of Crete. Minos had broken a promise to Poseidon by keeping a white bull meant for sacrifice, so the god made the queen, Pasiphaë, fall in love with the bull. Daedalus built her a hollow wooden cow, and from that union came the Minotaur, half man and half bull.

To hide his shame, Minos had Daedalus build the Labyrinth, a maze so tangled that even its maker could barely find the way out. The Minotaur was shut inside and fed young Athenians sent as tribute. In these years Daedalus also had a son: Icarus.

The Minotaur in the Labyrinth
Chapter Three · The Thread

A Secret Given Away

Years later the Athenian hero Theseus arrived to kill the Minotaur. Minos's daughter Ariadne fell in love with him and begged Daedalus for a way out of the maze. He gave her a simple answer: a ball of thread.

Theseus tied one end at the entrance and unwound it as he went. He slew the monster, followed the thread back to daylight, and sailed away with Ariadne. Only the Labyrinth's builder could have known the trick, and Minos knew it.

Chapter Four · The Tower

Feathers and Wax

Furious, Minos locked Daedalus and Icarus away and set guards on every harbour. "He may rule the land and the sea," Daedalus thought, looking up, "but not the sky."

He gathered feathers and laid them out from smallest to largest, like the reeds of a shepherd's pipe. He tied them at the middle with thread, sealed them at the base with wax, and bent them into a gentle curve. Icarus played at his feet, chasing feathers on the breeze and pressing the soft wax with his thumb, not knowing he was handling his own death.

Chapter Five · The Warning

The Middle Course

When the wings were finished, Daedalus strapped them to his son's shoulders. His hands shook and there were tears on his face as he gave the one rule that mattered.

"Fly the middle way. Too low, and the spray from the sea will soak your feathers and drag you down. Too high, and the sun will melt the wax. Stay between the two, follow me, and don't set your own course by the stars." Then he kissed the boy, a kiss he would never give again.

Between the sun and the sea
Chapter Six · The Flight

Higher and Higher

They rose together over the sea. Daedalus kept turning back to watch his son, like a bird coaxing a fledgling from the nest. Below, a fisherman with his rod, a shepherd on his staff and a farmer at his plough looked up and took them for gods.

They passed Samos, Delos and Paros. But the freedom went to Icarus's head. He left his father's path and climbed toward the open sky, closer and closer to the sun.

Chapter Seven · The Fall

The Wax Gives Way

So close to the sun, the wax softened and ran. The feathers slipped loose one by one and scattered on the wind. Icarus beat his bare arms, but they caught nothing but air.

He cried out his father's name as he fell, and the dark blue water closed over him. Daedalus, no longer a father, turned and called, "Icarus! Where are you?" Then he saw the feathers floating on the waves.

Chapter Eight · Icaria

A Grave by the Sea

Daedalus cursed his own skill. He pulled his son's body from the water and buried it on a nearby island, which has carried the boy's name ever since: Icaria. The sea around it is still called the Icarian Sea.

As he laid Icarus in the earth, a partridge watched from a muddy ditch and clapped its wings with delight. It was Perdix, the nephew Daedalus had thrown from the Acropolis long ago, now witness to his uncle's grief.

Chapter Nine · Sicily

The Spiral Shell

Daedalus flew on alone to Sicily, where King Cocalus took him in. Minos hunted him across the sea, carrying a spiral seashell and promising a reward to anyone who could pass a thread through it. Only Daedalus would be clever enough.

Cocalus handed the shell back threaded. Daedalus had tied the string to an ant and let it walk the spiral. Minos knew he had found his man, but Cocalus's daughters loved Daedalus for the toys he made them, and they killed Minos in his bath. Daedalus lived on and kept inventing. His son was gone forever.

Afterword

What the Myth Teaches

  • IHubris. A great gift used without limits destroys the one who holds it.
  • IIThe middle way. Too low is as dangerous as too high. The Greeks called this balance sophrosyne.
  • IIIListening. Youthful confidence ignored hard-won experience, and learned too late.
  • IVConsequences. The man who made a boy fall watched his own son fall.
  • VInvention. The cow, the maze and the wings each carried a cost. Cleverness needs conscience.
  • VIDaring. Some see Icarus as brave, not foolish. The failure was in judgement, not in wanting to fly.
  • VIIIndifference. In Bruegel's painting the ploughman keeps ploughing while Icarus drowns in a corner.

Freedom must be matched by the wisdom to use it. Ambition without limits, and genius without conscience, both end in a fall.
