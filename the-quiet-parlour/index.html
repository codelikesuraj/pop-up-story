The Quiet Parlour

A boy, his father, and the years after. A story about caring for a sick parent at a young age, being told to “man up”, and the grief that waits.

Turn the pages to watch each scene fold up. On the seventh page, pull the tab to help Yusuf with his first shave.

The parlour, in the evening
Part One · Daddy

Daddy

Yusuf’s father was the calmest man on their street.

He never shouted. He never hurried. When the neighbours argued about football after Jumu’ah, Daddy would lean back in his chair, smile, and say, “Ehn, we’ll see on Sunday.”

In the evenings, his old FM radio player sat beside him, playing his music: Brenda Fassie, Bob Marley, the same albums again and again. And whenever it was someone’s birthday in the house, Daddy played the same birthday song on that radio. Every year. Never a different one.

Yusuf was still small then, and he thought his father was the coolest person in the world.

Part One · Daddy

In and Out

Then Daddy got sick.

Not all at once. It came and went, like rain in the wet season. Some months he was in the hospital. Some months he was home, laughing at the TV like nothing had happened.

For as long as Yusuf could remember, at least two years, maybe more, the family lived like that: in and out, better and worse, hoping.

Part One · Daddy

Understanding

Nobody sat Yusuf down to explain. But he understood.

He understood when Mama’s voice went quiet on the phone. When his big sister Aisha called from university and asked, “How is he today?” When his other sister, Zainab, came back from the hospital with red eyes and said, “He’s fine, he’s fine.”

He noticed who came to help, too. Mama’s friend, Aunty Khadijah, was always there with food, with rides to the hospital, with a hand on Mama’s back.

Daddy’s own brothers and sisters hardly ever came at all.

Looking After Ahmad

So Yusuf did what he could. He looked after Ahmad.

They played football in the compound. They watched cartoons. When Ahmad asked if Daddy would come home soon, Yusuf said, “Yes. Soon.”

Part One · Daddy

Just the Two of Them

Sometimes Mama had to go out, and Zainab was away, and it was only Yusuf at home with Daddy.

Yusuf learned things a boy his age doesn’t usually learn. How to fold the pillows so Daddy could sit up. Which tablets came at which time. How to hold the cup steady when Daddy’s hands shook.

And when Daddy couldn’t make it to the bathroom on his own anymore, Yusuf helped him there, and cleaned him up afterwards, gently, without a word, and without ever once making a face.

“Thank you, my son,” Daddy would say quietly. And Yusuf would say, “It’s nothing, Daddy.”

Part One · Daddy

The First Shave

One afternoon, Daddy rubbed his chin and frowned. “Look at this bush,” he said. “Your mother will think I’ve become a goat.”

But his hands were shaking too much to hold the razor. So he handed it to Yusuf.

Yusuf had never shaved anything in his life. Not even his own face; there was nothing on it yet.

Part One · Daddy

Daddy Teaches Him

“Small strokes,” Daddy said. “Downwards first. Don’t press. Let the blade do the work.”

Yusuf’s hand trembled. Daddy covered it with his own. “Ehn, slowly. You want to leave me with no face?” Yusuf laughed, and his hand steadied.

Stroke by stroke, Daddy’s face came back: the face Yusuf knew. When it was done, Daddy touched his smooth chin and grinned. “See? A better barber than the one at the junction.”

So the first beard Yusuf ever shaved was his father’s.

“Small strokes. Downwards first.”

Part One · Daddy

Daddy Was Still Daddy

Even on the bad days, Daddy was still Daddy.

“You’re a better nurse than the ones at the hospital,” he’d say, and wink. Yusuf would roll his eyes. But he stayed a little longer every time.

Sometimes they didn’t talk at all. They just sat together, the two of them, as the evening adhan drifted in through the window.

Part Two · That Night

One Ordinary Night

The last night was an ordinary night.

Ahmad fell asleep during the cartoons. Zainab read in her room. Mama said goodnight.

Yusuf passed Daddy’s door on his way to bed. It was half open. Daddy was resting.

Yusuf almost went in. He didn’t.

Midnight

Something woke Yusuf in the middle of the night.

He didn’t know what. The house was quiet. Too quiet.

He lay still and listened. No snoring from Daddy’s room. Nothing at all.

He got out of bed.

Part Two · That Night

The Parlour

The light was on in the parlour.

Mama was there, in Daddy’s armchair. Aunty Khadijah sat close beside her, holding her hand. Mr. Ibrahim from next door stood by the window, his cap in his hands, saying nothing.

Nobody was crying. Nobody was talking.

The whole house was holding its breath.

Part Two · That Night

“Don’t.”

Yusuf turned towards Daddy’s room.

“Yusuf.” Mama’s voice cracked across the parlour, sharp in a way he had never heard. “Don’t go in there.”

He stopped with his hand on the door. He didn’t ask why. He didn’t need to.

In that moment, everything he’d been afraid of for so long became true.

He was twelve years old.

Part Two · That Night

Sunrise

Before dawn, the men came.

Aunty Khadijah took Yusuf and Ahmad to the boys’ room and closed the door. “Stay here,” she said gently. “Look after your brother.”

So Yusuf sat on the bed with Ahmad asleep against his shoulder, and listened. Low voices in the corridor. Water running. Footsteps going slowly out of the house, many of them together. Then the call for Fajr. Then quiet.

When the door finally opened, the sun was up, and Daddy was gone. Yusuf never saw him again.

He did not cry.

Part Two · That Night

The Relatives

By the time the men came back, the house was full of people. Aisha had rushed back from university. Neighbours brought mats and food. Voices murmured Inna lillahi wa inna ilayhi raji’un at the gate.

And Daddy’s brothers and sisters came, the ones who had hardly visited in all the time he was sick.

Mama greeted them politely. But that day, Aunty Khadijah never left the boys’ side. Whenever an aunt reached out for Ahmad, or an uncle started towards Yusuf, she was already there: smiling, polite, firm. “They’re resting,” she would say. Or, “They’ve just eaten.” Or, “Come, Yusuf, help me with these plates.”

Still, from across the compound, Yusuf heard one of the uncles say it loud enough for everyone: “That boy is the man of the house now. He should man up. His mother needs him to be strong.”

Yusuf kept his eyes on the plates. He didn’t say what he was thinking: Where were you? I’ve been strong all this time.

Part Three · The Man of the House

Something Changed

Yusuf never really cried. Not that day, not the next, not in the weeks after.

But the uncle’s words stayed with him, the way a stone stays in a shoe: You’re the man of the house now. Take care of your mother.

Somewhere in those weeks, something in Yusuf went quiet. The boy who used to laugh at cartoons and roll his eyes at Daddy’s jokes stepped back, and someone older stepped forward in his place.

He was still twelve. But he didn’t feel twelve anymore.

Part Three · The Man of the House

At the Gate

Now Mama had to be everything. Every morning before the sun was fully up, she tied her wrapper, gathered her goods, and went out to trade.

One morning, Yusuf woke early and stood at the gate to watch her go. At the corner, she turned and waved. She was smiling, but it was the tired smile he had started to notice.

If she has to carry all of it, he thought, then I will carry what I can.

That evening, he was waiting at the same gate when she came home. Before she could say a word, he took the bag from her hands. Mama raised an eyebrow. “Since when?” “Since today,” said Yusuf.

Part Three · The Man of the House

The Leaking Tap

Things in the house kept breaking. The kitchen tap dripped all night. When the plumber named his price, Mama went quiet in a way Yusuf didn’t like.

Yusuf had always been the boy who took things apart to see how they worked. That night, he took the tap apart. He laid every piece out in order on a cloth so he wouldn’t forget where it went, and he found the worn-out part. The next day he bought a new one with his own small savings.

When Mama came home, the kitchen was silent. No drip. “Who fixed this?” Ahmad pointed at his brother.

After that, it was the gate latch. A loose socket. The generator when it coughed and refused to start. Every repair was money Mama didn’t have to spend.

Part Three · The Man of the House

The First Birthday

Then came the first birthday in the house without Daddy. It was Ahmad’s. That morning, nobody knew quite what to do.

Then Zainab went into Daddy’s room and came back holding his old radio. She switched it on and played the song, the same birthday song Daddy had always played. For a moment, everyone smiled. It sounded exactly like before.

Then Mama’s smile wobbled. Zainab was laughing and crying at the same time. Ahmad didn’t know whether to dance. Yusuf stood by the door singing along under his breath, his throat tight. It was the happiest and the saddest few minutes the house had known.

At the next birthday, nobody brought the radio out. Nobody said why. The song just stopped, quietly, the way some things do.

Part Three · The Man of the House

Learning to Cook

Then Zainab went away to school, like Aisha before her, and there was no one left to cook when Mama came home late from trade days. The first evening, Mama came home exhausted and went straight to the kitchen. Yusuf watched her stand there, too tired to lift the pot.

The next trade day, Yusuf stood in that kitchen and decided. He burnt the first soup. The swallow came out lumpy and his arms ached from turning it. But he tried again. And again.

One evening, Mama came home late and smelled food. She stood in the kitchen doorway for a long time, looking at her son by the stove. Then she sat down and ate every bit of it.

“Who taught you this?” “Nobody,” said Yusuf. “Me.” Mama laughed, properly, for the first time in a long time. And she touched his head as she went to wash her hands.

Part Three · The Man of the House

The Line

At school, Yusuf was the boy teachers never had to worry about.

One afternoon, in the line for assembly, a bigger boy shoved him hard and said something about his father. Everyone turned to watch. Yusuf’s fists curled.

Then he thought: If I fight, they’ll call Mama. She’ll have to leave the market. She’ll lose a day’s sales. She’ll come here worried. He uncurled his fists and walked away. “Coward,” someone muttered. Yusuf let them say it.

That was how it was now. Before he did anything, he asked himself one question: What would this do to Mama? “Such a well-behaved boy,” the teachers said. Nobody asked if he was alright.

Part Four · The Years After

Uncle Bashir

In all those years, only one man really showed up.

Uncle Bashir, Mama’s younger brother, wasn’t loud about it. He just came. He called to ask about school. He sent what he could when money was tight. He sat with Mama on the veranda and talked through the grown-up things, so she didn’t have to carry them alone. His wife, Aunty Rukayya, always hugged Yusuf a little longer than she needed to, as if she knew.

And when Yusuf slipped, coming home too late, getting careless, speaking too sharply to Mama, it was Uncle Bashir who took him aside. He never shouted. He just said, “Your mother has enough to carry. Don’t add to it.”

Yusuf hated hearing it. He needed to hear it.

Part Four · The Years After

Going to University

The night before Yusuf left for university, Mama packed his bag herself, folding every shirt twice. In the morning, at the gate, she held his face in both hands and prayed over him quietly.

Then Uncle Bashir drove him there. He carried the bags up the stairs, checked the room and the lock on the door, and showed Yusuf where to buy food. Before he left, he pressed some money into Yusuf’s hand. “Call me if you need anything. Anything at all.”

The car hadn’t even left the hostel gate when Yusuf’s phone rang. It was Mama. “Have you eaten?”

Part Four · The Years After

Holidays

During holidays, Yusuf often stayed with Uncle Bashir and Aunty Rukayya. Their house was warm and loud. Uncle teased his children at dinner, fixed things around the house, and argued about football. Yusuf loved it there.

But some evenings, watching Uncle Bashir lean back in his chair, or his cousins run to the gate shouting “Daddy’s home!”, something would catch in his chest.

He would smile, and eat his food, and think of a quiet parlour far away. Later, he’d step outside and call Mama, just to hear her voice.

Part Four · The Years After

Out of Nowhere

Grief, Yusuf learned, did not come when you expected it.

It didn’t come at the funeral or in the weeks after. It came years later, out of nowhere. On an ordinary afternoon at school. Walking back from a lecture. Sitting alone in a quiet room.

Suddenly, it was all there again, as if it had been waiting.

Part Four · The Years After

The Ones Who Saw

Twice, he called his big sister Aisha, and when she picked up, he couldn’t speak at first. He just cried. Aisha didn’t ask him to explain. She didn’t tell him to be strong. She just stayed on the line and said, “I know. I know. I miss him too.”

And once, it happened in front of a friend. They were just talking, about nothing really, and then Yusuf wasn’t okay. His friend didn’t laugh. He didn’t look away. He just sat down beside him and waited until it passed.

Yusuf never forgot either of them.

Part Four · The Years After

When They Talk About Their Fathers

Sometimes, people talked about their fathers. My dad called this morning. My dad is coming for graduation. My dad said I should study this.

Yusuf would smile and nod and say nothing.

But inside, he’d be somewhere else: in a dim bedroom, a razor in his hand, his father’s hand resting over his.

Part Four · The Years After

Some Nights

And some nights, when the room was dark and no one was around, the tears came without asking.

No sobbing. No sound.

Just Yusuf, lying still, letting them fall in silence, the way he never let them fall when he was twelve.

Part Five · Carrying Him

Years Later

Years later, Yusuf stood in front of the bathroom mirror with a razor in his hand. There was finally something on his own chin to shave. He didn’t need anyone to show him how.

Small strokes. Downwards first. Don’t press. Let the blade do the work.

And as he worked, he heard Daddy’s voice, the way it sounded that afternoon in the bedroom: “Ehn, slowly.”

Yusuf stopped. He looked at his reflection. He thought of the boy who first held that razor, who had grown up so fast, who had never had the chance to go slowly at all. For a moment, he thought he saw Daddy’s grin looking back at him.

Part Five · Carrying Him

The Questions

These days, Yusuf carries things he doesn’t always know how to put down. Questions about work, and money, and what it means to provide. About choosing a wife, and how to be a good husband. About someday being a father himself, when his own was taken from him so early.

Things a man can only really ask another man, the one who went before him. Uncle Bashir helps where he can. Friends listen. But some questions were meant for Daddy. What would you do? Am I doing this right? Would you be proud of me?

And some questions have no answers at all. Did you know that night? Were you afraid? What would you have said, if I had gone in?

Yusuf asks them anyway, quietly, in his head. And the silence that answers is its own kind of grief.

Part Five · Carrying Him

Still Here

More than twelve years have passed now. Not everything was lost.

Mama is still here. She is still the first person he calls, and still the first to ask, “Have you eaten?” Aisha, Zainab and Ahmad are grown and busy, living their own lives, but they are still close. They still tease each other. They still show up. And Uncle Bashir is still here too, still pulling Yusuf aside when he needs it, even now.

Birthdays still come around, and the family still gathers. Nobody plays the song. But every year, for just a second, Yusuf still waits for it.

Part Five · Carrying Him

Carrying Him

Daddy’s old FM radio player is still with Yusuf. It moved with him from home to university to wherever life has taken him. He has never thought of letting it go. Some evenings, he switches it on and lets Brenda or Bob Marley fill the room.

The grief still visits sometimes. He has stopped waiting for it to go away.

You never really get over the ones who were there before. You just learn to carry them.

And some days, when he is calm while others are rushing, or patient with something broken, or smiling at an argument about football, Yusuf realises he has been carrying Daddy all along.
